# Defending the charge thesis

Prepared answers to the objections a sceptical scientist will raise about
NanoSolve's central claim. Written 2026-09-05, from a twelve-lens adversarial
review of the project's own specifications. Every citation below was checked
against the source, not quoted from memory.

Three audiences, one document:

- Martin, before a presentation or a conversation with a researcher.
- The app's future FAQ screen and the Study door.
- Anyone writing copy: if a claim is not defensible here, do not put it in
  the app.

## The thesis

> Nanoplastics carry an electrostatic charge. Friction renews that charge
> (the triboelectric effect), and charge is what lets these particles cross
> biological barriers, bind to proteins and lipids, and disrupt water and
> living tissue. Charge is the root cause. Particle count is only the symptom.

This is a mechanism claim. It is not yet a consensus position, and saying so
is a strength rather than a weakness: it is exactly the kind of claim a
student can help test, which is what the app asks them to do.

## The evidence stack

What is measured, in order of how directly it supports the thesis.

**Charge changes what a particle does to a barrier.** Neutral and
low-concentration anionic nanoparticles left the blood-brain barrier intact;
high-concentration anionic and all cationic nanoparticles disrupted it, and
cationic particles were immediately toxic at the barrier. Surface charge, not
size alone, decided the outcome. Lockman et al., *Journal of Drug Targeting*
2004, PubMed 15621689.

**Charge decides which proteins stick, and the coat decides passage.**
Unmodified polystyrene nanoplastics sit near −58 mV; amine-modified ones near
+64 mV, and the two gather different protein coronas in different amounts.
Corona composition is governed by size and surface properties including zeta
potential. So the corona is not a rival explanation to charge; it is
downstream of it. Cedervall et al., *PNAS* 2008; nanoplastic corona studies
in *Analytical and Bioanalytical Chemistry* and *Nanoscale* 2024.

**The coat then governs whether a particle reaches the brain.** In mice,
polystyrene nanoparticles reached the brain within two hours of oral dosing,
and only the nanometre sizes did. Cholesterol in the corona promoted passage
through the barrier membrane; a protein corona inhibited it. Kopatz et al.,
*Nanomaterials* 2023, PMC10141840.

**Charge outweighs corona in comparable systems.** Surface charge overrode
protein corona formation in determining cytotoxicity, cellular uptake and
biodistribution. *ACS Applied Bio Materials* 2025, PMC12175132.

**Charge decides dispersion in the environment.** Ionic strength screens the
surface charge of nanoplastics, and screened particles stop repelling each
other and aggregate. This is why nanoplastics clump in seawater and stay
dispersed in fresh water. Aggregation behaviour in the environment is charge
behaviour.

**Plastics charge by contact, and this is well characterised.** The
triboelectric series for common polymers is established well enough to sort
mixed plastic waste industrially by charge alone. Contact electrification also
occurs at liquid-solid interfaces, with charge transferred through an
intermediate water layer between polymer surfaces of different work function.
*Minerals Engineering* 2003; *Nature Communications* / PMC7979908 on
liquid-solid contact electrification.

**And the particles are in people, with an outcome signal.** Polyethylene was
found in the carotid plaque of 58% of 257 patients undergoing endarterectomy.
Over 34 months, patients with plastics in the plaque had roughly **4.5 times**
the risk of a composite of death, heart attack or stroke. Marfella et al.,
*New England Journal of Medicine* 2024, doi 10.1056/NEJMoa2309822.

## Objections, and how to answer them

### 1. "Static charge cannot persist in blood or seawater. They are electrolytes."

The strongest single objection, and the answer has three parts.

First, the quantity that matters in a fluid is not a static contact charge but
the **zeta potential**: the charge at the shear plane of a particle moving
through a liquid. It is routinely measured for nanoplastics, and it is not
zero.

Second, screening is not abolition. Raising ionic strength compresses the
double layer and reduces the effective repulsion; that is precisely why
nanoplastics aggregate in seawater. The observed aggregation is evidence that
charge is doing work, not evidence that it has gone.

Third, contact electrification does happen at liquid-solid interfaces, and the
mechanism has been studied at the level of electronic structure. Charge
transfer through an interposed water layer between polymers of different work
function is documented.

What to concede: "renewed by friction" is best evidenced in air and at
interfaces. Inside circulating blood, the honest formulation is that the
particle carries a surface charge that its environment modulates. Say that,
and the objection loses its force without costing you the thesis.

### 2. "The corona explains barrier crossing, not the charge."

Corona composition is itself determined by surface charge. Change the zeta
potential from −58 mV to +64 mV and you get a different corona, in different
quantity. The corona is the mechanism by which charge acts, not a competing
mechanism.

The useful framing: charge is upstream, corona is the intermediate step,
crossing is the outcome. Kopatz found cholesterol promotes and protein
inhibits, which is a corona result; what determines whether cholesterol or
protein dominates is the particle's surface.

### 3. "Charge cannot be the root cause. Size, shape and polymer all matter too."

Agreed, and the app should not claim charge is the only variable. The claim is
that charge is the most **general** lever: it is the property that is common
across polymers, that changes with weathering and friction, and that can
plausibly be engineered against.

That is a claim about leverage, not about exclusivity. Phrase it as: "of the
properties that decide where a nanoplastic goes, charge is the one you could
most plausibly intervene on." That is a research programme, and it is
defensible.

### 4. "Is any of this shown in humans?"

Yes, with a caveat you should raise yourself before anyone else does.
Marfella 2024 found plastics in more than half of carotid plaques and a 4.5x
hazard for death, heart attack or stroke over 34 months.

Concede first: it is observational, it cannot show causation, the authors say
so, and critics have noted that anti-contamination procedures were not fully
described. Then note that it is the strongest human-outcome dataset that
exists, published in the *New England Journal of Medicine*, and that no
comparable dataset points the other way.

### 5. "Your bottled-water number is contested."

It is, and the app shows the challenge. Qian et al. reported about 2.4 x 10^5
particles per litre, roughly 90% of them nanoplastics. Materić replied in
*PNAS* that every sample sat below the procedural blank and that the dominant
polyamide signal points to laboratory contamination. Qian replied in turn.

Answer: the app displays a band, tags the row "disputed", and shows both
sides. A number a student can interrogate builds more trust than a number
presented as settled. This is a feature of the design, not damage control.

### 6. "The tea-bag study was debunked."

Partly, and the part that survives still matters. Hernandez et al. reported
about 11.6 billion micro- and 3.1 billion nanoparticles per cup. The German
Federal Institute for Risk Assessment re-tested and concluded the sub-micron
population was largely precipitated oligomers, an artefact of drying.

But the same re-test still found **5,800 to 20,400 particles above one
micrometre per bag**. The dispute is about how many and how small, not about
whether a plastic tea bag sheds. The app shows the surviving number and labels
the rest as unresolved.

### 7. "Nanoparticle tracking analysis cannot tell plastic from anything else."

Correct, and this is why the app carries a "counted, could be more than
plastic" tag rather than presenting every count as confirmed plastic. The
microwave-container and paper-cup sub-micron figures both carry it.

This is the same weakness the regulator used to challenge the tea-bag paper,
and applying the tag consistently is what makes the tagging honest rather than
decorative.

### 8. "You are adding counts from five different instruments. That ranking is meaningless."

A fair criticism of most exposure calculators, and the app is built to avoid
it. Rows are grouped into families by the size floor of the instrument that
produced them, and rows in different families are never summed. There is no
grand total and no percentage anywhere in the app.

The app then offers a second view by estimated mass, which is the only
comparable quantity, and the two orders disagree: microwaved plastic leads by
count, a plastic cutting board leads by weight. That disagreement is taught
rather than hidden, with the caption "a bigger bar can mean a better
microscope, not more plastic".

### 9. "Nobody knows what dose is harmful, so the numbers are alarmism."

The app says exactly that, on the same screen as the numbers. It shows what is
known, what is not known, and states that no study has tied a number like the
student's to an illness.

The design principle behind this is evidenced: fear appeals work only when
paired with an efficacy statement, and fear without a workable response lowers
self-efficacy rather than raising action. Tannenbaum et al., *Psychological
Bulletin* 2015, 127 studies. Every frightening number in the app sits beside
something the student could actually do.

### 10. "The regional data is thin."

True, and it is one of the app's better arguments. Kuwait has an indoor
aerosol baseline of 3.2 to 27.1 particles per cubic metre. Bushehr in Iran has
inhalation figures for normal and dusty days. Western Arabian Gulf fish have
been surveyed. Saudi tap and bottled water have been screened, though only
down to 25 micrometres.

Nobody has counted the nano fraction in Gulf tap water, in a 20 litre cooler
jug, or in the air of a Gulf city. The app names these as open questions with
a size and a next step, because a student with a term project could genuinely
close one.

### 11. "Charge neutralisation as a solution is speculative."

It is, and the leaderboard treats it as a hypothesis to be developed rather
than a finished answer. The honest framing: if charge governs where particles
go, then managing charge is the most general intervention point, and the
project exists to find out whether that is true.

Anyone who dismisses the whole idea should be asked what they would intervene
on instead. There is no competing general lever on offer.

### 12. "Why should a student trust an app over a journal?"

They should not, and the app never asks them to. Every number carries its
source, its method, its size floor and its caveats, and tapping a bar opens
the citation. The app's job is to get a student to the literature, not to
replace it.

## What would confirm the thesis, and what would falsify it

Worth saying out loud in any presentation, because a claim that cannot be
tested is not science.

Confirming evidence would look like: a dose-response relationship between
particle zeta potential and barrier crossing in a human tissue model; a
demonstration that neutralising the charge on environmental nanoplastics
reduces uptake in a living system; charge measurements on particles recovered
from human tissue rather than laboratory-synthesised spheres.

Falsifying evidence would look like: barrier crossing that tracks size or
polymer chemistry while charge is held constant; recovered particles whose
surface charge is fully screened in vivo with no measurable effect; a
neutralisation intervention that changes nothing.

The project should say which of these it is pursuing.
